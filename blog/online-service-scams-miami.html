Online Service Scams in Miami: When You Pay for Work That Never Comes

📅 November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read
Back to Blog

One of the most common fraud patterns in Miami — and among the least reported — is service fraud. Someone is hired to build a website, produce photos or video, design a logo, run social media, write copy, or provide business consulting. They collect partial or full payment upfront. And then they deliver nothing, or something so far below what was promised that it might as well be nothing.

Unlike a romance scam or a rental scam, service fraud doesn't feel dramatic from the outside. There's no fake persona, no manufactured emergency. Just a person who appeared professional, made promises, took money, and either vanished or produced work that wasn't worth a fraction of what was paid. It happens constantly in Miami, to small business owners especially, and it rarely makes headlines because the individual amounts are often just below the threshold where people feel it's worth pursuing.

🚨 Why Service Fraud Is So Common — and So Hard to Prosecute

Service fraud occupies a legal gray zone that bad actors exploit deliberately. The core challenge is proving intent: did this person intentionally defraud you, or were they simply incompetent, overwhelmed, or disorganized? Courts treat these very differently, and the line between a failed business relationship and criminal fraud is genuinely blurry.

Several factors make service fraud especially prevalent in Miami:

  • No physical product means no clear evidence of theft. A contractor who takes your deposit and disappears has clearly taken your money. A web designer who takes your deposit and produces unusable work has also taken your money, but the legal path to recovery is significantly more complicated.
  • Perpetrators routinely change business names. After burning clients under one name, Miami service fraudsters frequently dissolve their LLC, form a new one, and restart with a fresh reputation. The person is the same; the entity is different; the fraud cycle begins again.
  • Amounts are strategically small. Service scammers often keep individual deposit amounts low enough — a few hundred to a few thousand dollars — that victims weigh the hassle of legal action against the amount owed and decide it's not worth it. The scammer knows this. The business model depends on it.
  • Digital services are inherently subjective. What constitutes a "complete" website, a "professional" logo, or "effective" social media management is harder to define than a roof or a tiled floor. This ambiguity gives bad actors room to claim they delivered what was agreed.

📋 Common Service Fraud Patterns in Miami

Web Design and Digital Marketing Ghost Jobs

A web designer or digital marketing agency is hired, collects a deposit or full payment, provides a few early deliverables to demonstrate activity, and then disappears or becomes increasingly unresponsive. The website is never finished; the marketing campaign never launches; the deliverables that were provided are barely functional. The client has spent money they cannot recover on a business that is now untraceable.

Photography and Videography Deposit Disappearances

A photographer or videographer collects a deposit to secure a date for a wedding, product shoot, event, or brand content day. The day arrives and the photographer either doesn't show up, shows up briefly and delivers nothing usable, or delivers raw files months later that are unusable. Meanwhile they've moved on to new clients and new deposits. This is especially painful for weddings, where the event cannot be re-staged and the photos are irreplaceable.

Social Media Management Scams

A "social media manager" is retained on a monthly basis to run a business's Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok presence. They post inconsistently, deliver no meaningful analytics, make promises about growth and engagement that never materialize, and become impossible to reach when the client wants to cancel and recover their content and account access. Sometimes the client's accounts are held hostage — the manager has the login credentials and won't relinquish them without a fight.

Business Consulting with No Deliverables

A "business consultant," "brand strategist," or "growth advisor" is engaged for a significant monthly retainer. They participate in a few calls, produce a PowerPoint deck or a brief written document, and then stop producing meaningful output. The engagement drags on while the client waits for the value that was promised, afraid to terminate the relationship because they've already paid and are hoping the investment will eventually pay off.

Before you hire anyone for a digital service in Miami: Search their full name AND their business name on Google with the word "complaints" and separately with "scam." Search their business name on SunBiz.org to confirm the entity exists and is active. And search their name on MiamiScammers.com to see if they've been reported by prior clients. Five minutes of research can prevent months of frustration.

✅ How to Hire Service Providers Safely

These practices don't eliminate the risk entirely, but they eliminate most of it:

  • Demand a detailed scope of work before you pay anything. The contract should specify exactly what will be delivered, in what format, by what date. Vague language like "social media support" or "website assistance" is not a scope of work. If the provider can't articulate specifically what they're delivering, they may not know either.
  • Structure payments around milestones, not time. Pay for completed deliverables, not for hours worked or time elapsed. A web designer should receive 25 percent when the wireframe is approved, 25 percent when the first functional build is delivered, 25 percent when revisions are complete, and 25 percent upon launch — not 50 percent upfront and 50 percent whenever they get around to finishing.
  • Verify they have real prior clients you can actually speak to. Ask for two or three references and call them. Not an email testimonial on the provider's website — an actual phone call with a real person who hired them and can speak to what was delivered and when.
  • Use a credit card whenever possible. Credit card chargebacks are your most powerful recovery tool if a service provider fails to deliver. You have up to 60–120 days from the transaction to initiate a dispute, and credit card companies generally side with the consumer when no service was delivered. Never pay the full amount upfront via Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, or wire transfer if you can avoid it.
  • Get a personal guarantee if the provider is operating as an LLC. An LLC with no assets provides effectively zero protection to you as a client. If the LLC dissolves — which is easy and cheap in Florida — your legal claim goes with it. A contract that includes the owner's personal name and personal liability gives you someone to pursue if the entity disappears.
  • Retain access to all accounts and assets throughout the engagement. Never give a service provider exclusive control of your website, your social media accounts, or your domain name. You should retain admin access to everything, with the provider operating as a secondary user. This prevents the account-hostage scenario that is common in social media management disputes.

LLC protection only protects the owner, not you: When a Miami service provider operating as an LLC takes your money and delivers nothing, you are suing the LLC — not the person. If that LLC has no assets and no active contracts, your judgment is essentially worthless. Getting the owner's personal name on the contract, with a clause holding them personally liable for non-delivery, is the single most important protection you have when dealing with solo operators and small agencies.

If a Service Provider in Miami Scammed You

  • Send a formal written demand letter via certified mail first. Before filing anything, send a demand letter to the provider's registered business address and their personal address if you have it. State clearly what was agreed, what was not delivered, and the specific amount you are demanding back, with a deadline of 10–14 days. This creates a legal record and sometimes produces results without further action.
  • File a Florida AG consumer fraud complaint at myfloridalegal.com. The Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act (FDUTPA) covers exactly this type of fraud. Attorney General complaints create a public record and can trigger investigations if patterns emerge.
  • File a BBB complaint. While the BBB cannot force a resolution, negative complaints on the public record can damage a fraudster's ability to find new clients — and are searchable by anyone doing due diligence.
  • File in Miami-Dade Small Claims Court for amounts up to $8,000. You don't need an attorney. File at the Clerk of Courts, pay the filing fee, and serve the defendant at their registered business or personal address. Miami-Dade small claims judgments can be enforced against bank accounts.
  • Consult a civil attorney for larger amounts. If the amount is above $8,000, or if you believe the provider defrauded multiple clients using the same scheme, a civil litigation attorney can assess whether a lawsuit is worth pursuing. Some attorneys take fraud cases on contingency if the evidence is strong.
  • Submit a report to MiamiScammers.com. Name, business name, social media handles, and what happened — with as much documentation as possible. Future clients who search their name before hiring will find your report.

Service fraud is preventable. The providers who defraud clients repeatedly rely on the fact that most victims don't report, don't pursue legal action, and don't warn others. Breaking that silence — through complaint filings, reviews, and public reports — is the most effective tool the Miami community has against repeat offenders.

If you've been defrauded by a service provider in Miami, submit a report to MiamiScammers.com. It takes ten minutes and can protect the next small business owner from going through the same thing.
