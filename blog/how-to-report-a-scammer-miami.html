How to Report a Scammer in Miami (And Actually Get Results)

📅 October 2026 ⏱ 6 min read
Back to Blog

Most people who get scammed never report it. Some feel embarrassed. Some assume nothing will happen. Some don't know where to go. The result is that the same scammer moves on to the next victim, and the one after that, because nobody created a paper trail.

Reporting matters — even when you don't get your money back. Law enforcement agencies build cases from patterns. One report might not trigger an investigation. Ten reports about the same person almost certainly will. Here is every place you should report, what each one does, and how to write a report that actually gets read.

Step 1 — File a Police Report with Miami-Dade Police

This is the most important step and the one people skip most often because they assume the police "won't do anything about a small amount." File anyway. Here's why:

  • A police report creates an official record with a case number. That case number is required by many other agencies before they'll act.
  • If the scammer has targeted others, detectives may already be building a case. Your report could be the one that pushes it over the threshold for an arrest.
  • For civil court, insurance claims, and tax deductions of fraud losses, you often need a police report number.

How to file: Call Miami-Dade Police non-emergency at 305-476-5423, or go in person to any district station. For online fraud under $10,000, many jurisdictions accept a report by phone. Ask for a copy of your report and the case number in writing.

If the scammer is in another county or state, you can still file with Miami-Dade if that's where you are. The report documents that a crime occurred against a Miami resident, which gives local detectives jurisdiction to investigate.

Step 2 — Florida Attorney General's Office

The Florida AG's Consumer Protection Division investigates businesses and individuals engaged in deceptive practices. They can issue cease-and-desist orders, pursue civil penalties, and refer cases for criminal prosecution. They track complaint patterns — if multiple people report the same person or business, the AG can take action even when a single police report wouldn't trigger anything.

  • File online: myfloridalegal.com → "File a Complaint"
  • Best for: Business fraud, contractor scams, rental fraud, any case involving a Florida-registered business
  • What to include: Full name of the person/business, any LLC names, the contract or agreement, payment receipts, all communications

Step 3 — Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

The FTC is the federal agency responsible for consumer fraud. They don't investigate individual cases, but they feed reports into a national database used by law enforcement at every level — local, state, and federal. When agencies see a pattern of reports tied to the same name, phone number, or email, it triggers investigations.

  • File online: reportfraud.ftc.gov
  • Best for: Any type of fraud, especially online scams, impersonation, and identity theft
  • After you file: The FTC will give you a personalized recovery plan based on the type of fraud you experienced

Step 4 — FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

If your scam involved the internet — email, social media, a website, online marketplace, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency — report it to the FBI's IC3. This is the primary agency for online fraud, romance scams, business email compromise, and investment fraud. The IC3 shares reports with FBI field offices and can initiate federal investigations for large-scale operations.

  • File online: ic3.gov
  • Best for: Romance scams, online fraud, wire fraud, crypto fraud, business email compromise
  • Note: IC3 accepts reports regardless of dollar amount. Small amounts matter because they help build the pattern for large cases.

Step 5 — Better Business Bureau (BBB) Scam Tracker

The BBB Scam Tracker is a public database where anyone can submit and read scam reports. It's searchable by name, location, and scam type. Unlike law enforcement reports, BBB reports are public — meaning other potential victims searching the scammer's name may find your report and be protected.

  • File online: bbb.org/scamtracker
  • Best for: Any type of scam. Reports are public and SEO-indexed, meaning they show up in Google searches for the scammer's name.
  • Also file a Business Review if the scammer operates under a business name — negative reviews on BBB rank in Google and warn future victims.

Step 6 — Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)

If the fraud involved a financial product — a loan, a bank account, a payment app, a mortgage, or a credit card — report it to the CFPB as well. The CFPB has enforcement authority over financial companies and can act when those companies are used to facilitate fraud.

  • File online: consumerfinance.gov/complaint
  • Best for: Fraud involving Zelle, wire transfers, fintech apps, predatory loans, or mortgage fraud

Step 7 — MiamiScammers.com

Community reporting fills the gap that official channels miss. When someone has scammed people but hasn't been formally charged, they have a clean record in every government database. A report on MiamiScammers.com puts their name and face in front of people who are about to be their next victim.

  • Submit a report: miamiscammers.com → Submit a Report
  • What to include: Full name, any aliases, photo if available, description of what happened, and any public records you found
  • Impact: Reports are reviewed and published with public records. They are indexed by Google and show up when someone searches the scammer's name.

How to Write a Report That Gets Results

Vague reports get filed and forgotten. Detailed reports get acted on. Here's what every report should include:

  1. Full legal name of the person — not a nickname. Include any aliases or business names.
  2. Contact information you have: phone numbers, email addresses, social media profiles, website URLs.
  3. Dates — when first contact was made, when money was paid, when communication stopped.
  4. Exact dollar amount paid and the method used (cash, wire, Zelle, crypto, check, gift cards).
  5. What was promised vs. what was delivered. Be specific. "He said he would build two websites by June 1st. As of October, nothing has been delivered and he has stopped responding."
  6. Evidence you have: Screenshots of texts, emails, contracts, payment receipts, social media profiles. Attach everything.
  7. Any witnesses or other victims you know of. If you found others who were scammed by the same person, include their contact information with their permission.

Do not wait. Reports filed immediately after the fraud are taken more seriously and investigated more aggressively. Evidence — phone numbers, email accounts, social media profiles — disappears quickly once a scammer knows they've been caught. File everything within 24–48 hours if possible.

Quick Reference — Where to Report Fraud in Miami

  • Miami-Dade Police (non-emergency): 305-476-5423
  • Florida Attorney General: myfloridalegal.com
  • FTC (all fraud types): reportfraud.ftc.gov
  • FBI Internet Crime (online fraud): ic3.gov
  • BBB Scam Tracker (public database): bbb.org/scamtracker
  • CFPB (financial fraud): consumerfinance.gov/complaint
  • Community reporting: miamiscammers.com

File everywhere. These agencies share data with each other. Filing in multiple places doesn't create duplicate work — it increases the chance your report lands on the desk of the person with the authority to act on it. It takes 30 extra minutes and could mean a scammer faces real consequences instead of moving on to their next victim.
