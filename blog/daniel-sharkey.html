Daniel Sharkey: Polished Events, Long Rap Sheet

📅 November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read
Back to Blog

The Setup Looks Legitimate

Daniel Sharkey operates in the Miami event space through his company, Aurum Nexus Global LLC. The brand looks professional: private capital forums for wealthy attendees, venues chosen for their exclusivity, materials that project authority and insider access.

His most visible production, the New Horizons Private Capital Forum, draws the kind of crowd that assumes a certain level of due diligence already happened before they walked in the door. The room has money in it. The branding looks expensive. The host presents himself as a trusted organizer.

That presentation is the product. It is not the vetting.

🚨 Polish Is Cheap

A branded event requires a rented venue, a printing company, and a website. That is not evidence of legitimacy. It is evidence of a marketing budget.

A room full of wealthy attendees is not vetting — it is a sales environment. Everyone in that room is operating on the same assumption: that someone else already checked. No one actually checked.

This is a known pattern. The more prestigious a setting looks, the more participants suspend their own verification instincts. The more money in the room, the more each person assumes the host was vetted by someone with more resources or authority than they have. The gap between assumed due diligence and actual due diligence is exactly where this kind of fraud operates.

None of this is a secret. It is all publicly accessible to anyone who took 10 minutes to look before writing a check or wiring a deposit.

Why Wealthy Audiences Are the Target

The more exclusive an event appears, the more each attendee assumes someone else performed due diligence. The organizer assumes the attendees vetted the host. The attendees assume the organizer did. No one actually did.

High-end branding signals legitimacy to people who have more money than time — people accustomed to environments where someone in an institution already filtered out the bad actors. Private capital forums, in particular, attract individuals who are sophisticated about finance but are operating in a social setting, not a transactional one. The guard is down.

This is not an accident. The venue, the branding, and the audience composition are all part of the pitch. The same people who would never wire money to an unsolicited email will hand over a deposit to someone in a well-lit hotel ballroom without looking anything up.

✅ How to Actually Check an Event Host

If someone is asking you for a deposit, a sponsorship fee, a vendor contract, or a table purchase — here is what you do before paying:

  1. Search Miami-Dade County court records. Go to the Clerk of the Courts public portal at miami-dadeclerk.com. Search the host's full legal name. This takes under five minutes and costs nothing. It will return any civil or criminal case filings in the county.
  2. Verify the business entity. Search the company name in the Florida Division of Corporations database at search.sunbiz.org. Check when it was registered, who the registered agent is, and whether it is active and in good standing. A legitimate business entity takes seconds to verify.
  3. Call the venue directly. Look up the venue's phone number independently and call them. Ask whether the event is contracted and whether the deposit has been received. Legitimate venues can confirm contracted events. If the venue has no record of the event, that is your answer.
  4. Ask for references from past vendors. A host who has successfully produced events has vendors who were paid. Caterers, AV companies, decorators — people who worked the event. If they cannot produce a single vendor who will vouch for them on the record, that is an answer.
  5. Search their name before you pay. A quick search of "[Name] + Miami + scam" or "[Name] + court records" takes two minutes. Reports like this one are designed to surface when you do that search.
  6. Get refund terms in writing, in the contract. If the event cancels — for any reason — what happens to your money? If the contract does not answer this question clearly and specifically, do not sign it. No exceptions.

The basic rule: Polish is not vetting. A beautiful venue is not a background check. A room full of wealthy people is not proof that anyone did the work. Do the work yourself — it takes less time than recovering from a bad contract.
