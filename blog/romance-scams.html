Romance Scams: How Scammers Use Fake Love to Steal Real Money

📅 October 2026 ⏱ 6 min read
Back to Blog

Romance scams are among the most devastating forms of fraud — not just financially, but emotionally. The scammer doesn't just take your money. They take weeks or months of your life, your trust, and your sense of judgment along with it. In Florida, romance scams cost victims tens of millions of dollars every year, and South Florida is one of the most targeted regions in the country.

These scams don't only happen to people who are lonely or naive. They happen to sharp, educated, successful people who simply met someone charming online. Understanding how the playbook works is the first step to protecting yourself.

🚨 How Romance Scams Work

1. The Initial Contact

Romance scammers find victims on dating apps, social media, Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, and even church or community groups. They create profiles using stolen photos — often of attractive military personnel, doctors, engineers, or successful-looking professionals. The profiles are polished and convincing. They reach out first, showering the target with attention and compliments almost immediately.

Red flag: They found you first. They're unusually attractive and successful on paper. They compliment you heavily within the first few messages. This is called "love bombing" — and it's a deliberate technique.

2. The Relationship Build-Up

The scammer invests real time. Days, weeks, sometimes months of daily texts, calls, and emotional conversations. They ask about your life, your family, your dreams. They share their own (fabricated) struggles and vulnerabilities to create intimacy. They say exactly what you want to hear. They want you emotionally invested before they ever ask for anything.

They will almost always claim to be working abroad — on an oil rig, deployed in the military, working a medical contract overseas, or traveling for business. This is why they can never meet in person and why video calls are always conveniently impossible or glitchy.

3. The Ask

Once trust is established, a crisis appears. It's always urgent and always just beyond what they can cover themselves: a medical emergency, a legal problem, a business deal that needs a bridge payment, a fee to receive a large inheritance, or customs fees to release a package they're sending you. The first ask is often small — just enough to test whether you'll help. Each subsequent ask gets larger.

The pattern: Every crisis is solvable with money — your money. The scammer always has a reason they can't use their own funds, access their own accounts, or wait for a transfer. And the solution always creates a bigger emergency if you don't act immediately.

4. The Crypto and Wire Transfer Push

Romance scammers almost never ask for money through traceable channels like Venmo or PayPal. They push for wire transfers, gift cards (read the card numbers to them over the phone), or cryptocurrency. These payment methods are designed to be irreversible. Once you send it, it's gone. There is no dispute process, no chargeback, and no recovery.

5. The Pig Butchering Variant

A newer and particularly devastating version of romance fraud is called "pig butchering." The scammer builds a romantic relationship, then casually introduces a "great investment opportunity" — usually a crypto trading platform that they've supposedly been making money on. They'll show you screenshots of profits. They'll even let you "withdraw" small amounts early on to build confidence. Once you've invested a significant sum, the platform freezes, fees are demanded to release funds, and eventually everything disappears along with the scammer.

Know this: Any romantic interest who steers the conversation toward investments, crypto platforms, or trading apps — no matter how gradually — is almost certainly running a scam. Legitimate romantic partners do not double as financial advisors.

⚠️ Warning Signs — The Red Flag Checklist

  • They can never meet in person. They're always overseas, always traveling, always have a reason why now isn't a good time.
  • Video calls never work properly. The camera is broken, the connection is bad, the call drops every time. Real people can video chat.
  • They profess love or deep feelings very quickly. Within days or a few weeks of meeting online, before you've ever seen each other in person.
  • Their photos look too good. Run any photo through Google Images or TinEye reverse image search. Scammers steal photos from real people's social media accounts.
  • Their profile or story doesn't quite add up. Inconsistencies in their background, job details that don't make sense, vague answers when pressed.
  • They move communication off the platform quickly. They want to switch to text, WhatsApp, or Telegram right away — away from any platform that monitors for scam activity.
  • They ask for money. Any request for financial help from someone you've never met in person is a scam. 100% of the time.
  • They ask for gift cards. No legitimate person in a legitimate emergency asks to be paid in Google Play or iTunes gift cards.
  • They discourage you from talking to friends or family about them. Isolation is a tactic. Scammers know that people outside the relationship will see it more clearly than you can from inside it.

Do this immediately: Reverse image search every photo they've sent you. Go to images.google.com, click the camera icon, and upload the photo. If the same face appears under a different name anywhere online, you're talking to a scammer.

✅ How to Protect Yourself

  1. Never send money to someone you haven't met in person. No matter how long you've been talking. No matter how real it feels. No matter what they say the emergency is. This is the single rule that will protect you from 100% of romance scams.
  2. Insist on a live video call before the relationship gets serious. Not a recorded video — a live call where you can ask them to hold up a specific object or do something in real time. If they can't or won't do this, you are not talking to the person in the photos.
  3. Talk to someone you trust about this person. Share the story with a friend or family member. People on the outside can often see what you can't when you're emotionally involved.
  4. Never send gift cards, wire transfers, or crypto to a romantic interest. Legitimate emergencies have legitimate solutions. No one who cares about you needs you to drain your savings account to help them.
  5. Be especially cautious about investment advice from a romantic interest. If they introduce any kind of trading platform, crypto opportunity, or financial scheme, stop the relationship immediately.
  6. Search their name, email, and phone number. A quick search can reveal if the same person has been reported as a scammer elsewhere. The FTC's fraud database and the FBI's IC3 portal are good starting points.
  7. Trust the feeling that something is off. Scammers are skilled at making you doubt your instincts. If something feels wrong, it usually is.

If You've Already Been Targeted

If you believe you're being scammed — or have already sent money — do not feel ashamed. These operations are run by organized criminal networks with teams of people whose full-time job is to manipulate victims. It happens to smart, careful people every day. What matters now is what you do next.

  • Stop all contact and stop sending money immediately. Every additional payment makes recovery less likely, not more.
  • File a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. Romance fraud is a federal crime, and the IC3 shares reports with law enforcement agencies that investigate these networks.
  • File a complaint with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. The FTC tracks fraud patterns and uses these reports to pursue enforcement actions.
  • Contact your bank immediately if you sent a wire transfer. In some cases — especially within 24–72 hours — banks can attempt a recall. Success is not guaranteed, but the faster you act, the better the chance.
  • If you sent cryptocurrency, contact the exchange you used. Crypto transactions are generally irreversible, but some exchanges have fraud departments that can flag wallets and potentially assist with investigations.
  • Submit a report to MiamiScammers.com if the person is based in or operating out of Miami. Sharing the name, photos, and contact information they used helps warn others who may be in the early stages of being targeted by the same scammer.

You are not alone: The FTC reported that Americans lost over $1.3 billion to romance scams in a single recent year — more than any other fraud category. If this happened to you, you are one of hundreds of thousands of victims. There are support communities, counselors who specialize in fraud recovery, and law enforcement resources specifically for this. Reach out.
