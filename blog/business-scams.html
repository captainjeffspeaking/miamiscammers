Business Scams: Why a Contract Isn't Enough — and What You Must Do Before You Pay

📅 October 2026 ⏱ 6 min read
Back to Blog

A signed contract feels like protection. It isn't — not on its own. Business scammers know exactly how the legal system works, and many of them build their entire operation around the gaps. If you're hiring someone for a service, working with a freelancer, or entering any paid business arrangement, read this first.

🚨 The $8,000 Trap — And Why Scammers Love It

In Florida, Small Claims Court handles disputes up to $8,000. Above that, you're in County Court — and you almost certainly need a lawyer. Attorney retainers alone often run $3,000–$5,000 or more, and litigation can drag on for months.

Scammers know this math better than you do. Many intentionally keep their takes under $8,000 — sometimes right at $5,000 or $6,000 — because they've calculated that it's not worth your time or money to chase them. Even in Small Claims Court, a judgment in your favor means nothing if you can't collect. If they've moved on, dissolved their LLC, or have no assets in their name, a judgment is just paper.

The cold reality: Getting scammed out of $5,000 often costs more to fight legally than it does to absorb the loss. Scammers count on exactly that. Prevention is your only real protection.

📄 Why Contracts With LLCs Can Be Worthless

Signing a contract with an LLC sounds official. But an LLC is only as good as what's behind it — and savvy scammers use LLCs as a wall between you and them personally.

  • The LLC may no longer exist. Florida LLCs must file an annual report and pay a fee to stay active. Many scammers let their LLC lapse — or dissolve it after the job — leaving you holding a contract with an entity that legally no longer exists.
  • The LLC may have no assets. Even if the LLC is active, it may have zero bank accounts, no property, and nothing to collect from. A judgment against a shell LLC is uncollectable.
  • New LLCs are easy to form. A scammer who burns one LLC can form a new one the next day under a slightly different name. They repeat the cycle. Check how old the LLC is before you sign anything.
  • The person behind the LLC may not be personally liable. One of the main purposes of an LLC is to shield the owner from personal liability. If a scammer operates entirely through an LLC and you can't pierce the corporate veil, your contract may not touch their personal bank account or property.

Always verify: Search any LLC on Florida's Division of Corporations site at search.sunbiz.org. Check the formation date, the registered agent, and whether it's in "Active" status. A company formed last month with a P.O. box as its address is a serious red flag.

📍 If You Can't Serve Them, You Can't Sue Them

This is the part most people don't think about until it's too late. To take someone to court — even Small Claims — you have to serve them with legal papers. That requires a known address. If all you have is an email address, a phone number, and an LLC name, you may have no way to serve the person.

Florida allows substitute service on an LLC's registered agent, but scammers often use registered agent services with no real connection to them. Personal service — showing up at their home — requires you to know where they live. If they gave you a business address that's a coworking space or a mailbox, you're stuck.

Bottom line: Before you sign a contract, make sure you have a home address for the person you're doing business with. Not a P.O. box. Not just a business address. An actual residential address — because that's what you'll need if things go wrong.

✅ Before You Sign — The Business Contract Checklist

  1. Get a copy of their government-issued photo ID. This isn't optional. Any legitimate professional will understand the request. If they refuse or make excuses, walk away. A driver's license tells you their legal name and their address at the time of issuance.
  2. Get their home address in writing. Not just a business address — a residential address. Include it in the contract itself. If they won't provide one, that's your answer.
  3. Verify any LLC before you pay a dollar. Pull up the company on search.sunbiz.org. Check when it was formed, confirm it's in Active status, and look at who the registered agent and officers are. Ask yourself: does this look like a real company?
  4. Never pay a large deposit upfront. A standard arrangement for any project is a small deposit to begin (10–25%), a milestone payment mid-project, and the remainder on completion. Anyone who demands 50% or 100% upfront before producing anything has very little incentive to deliver.
  5. Tie payments to deliverables — in the contract. "Payment due upon project completion" is vague. Spell out exactly what must be delivered, by when, and in what form before each payment is released. Make it specific and measurable.
  6. Do not pay the final balance until you have the finished product in hand. This seems obvious, but scammers are skilled at creating urgency — "I need the last payment to release the files," "My server costs are due." Hold the final payment until you have everything you paid for.
  7. Build in a timeline and consequences for missing it. A contract without a deadline is unenforceable in any meaningful way. Include specific due dates and language about what happens if they're missed — whether that's a refund, a reduced fee, or termination of the agreement.
  8. Keep every communication in writing. Don't let important conversations happen only by phone. Follow up calls with an email: "As discussed, you agreed to deliver X by Y date." If it ever comes to court, texts and emails are evidence. A phone call is your word against theirs.
  9. Search their name before you hire them. A quick Google search, a Florida court records search, and a visit to sites like MiamiScammers.com can reveal a pattern you'd never see from a single interaction. Scammers rely on each victim not knowing about the previous ones.

If It Goes Wrong

If someone has taken your money and failed to deliver — or disappeared — here's your path forward:

  • File a police report immediately. Theft of services and contract fraud are criminal matters. A police report creates an official record and may trigger an investigation, especially if others have filed reports against the same person.
  • File a complaint with the Florida Attorney General at myfloridalegal.com. The AG's office tracks patterns of fraud and can take action against repeat offenders.
  • Small Claims Court (for amounts up to $8,000) is relatively fast and inexpensive — filing fees are under $100 in most cases, and you don't need a lawyer. But you must be able to serve the defendant at a known address.
  • Florida's Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act (FDUTPA) may allow you to recover attorney's fees in addition to damages if a business misled you. Consult an attorney if the amount is significant.
  • Submit a report to MiamiScammers.com so the next person doesn't have to learn the same lesson you did.

The single most important thing: Slow down. Scammers create artificial urgency — a "limited time" offer, a project that has to start immediately, a deal that disappears if you ask too many questions. Any legitimate professional will give you time to verify who they are. If someone is pressuring you to sign and pay before you've done your homework, that pressure itself is the warning sign.
